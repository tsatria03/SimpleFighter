SimpleFighter — the V1 era (1.0 → 1.9)

V1 is the story of the game becoming a platform instead of just a game. It opened, on 6/26/2024, as a survival arena shooter with hard-coded content — a 50-object performance cap to tame the lag, bouncing and dodgeable bullets, vehicles, HRTF and reverb audio, an upgrade points menu, and an object info menu that was the distant ancestor of today's spier. Fun, but everything was baked into the code.

The real work of V1 was pulling all that content out of the code and into editable files. Characters came first, then weapons — each given its own attribute file, with the parsers to read them, until all four weapon families (melee, explosive, artillery, archery) and every character stat were authored on disk rather than compiled in. Alongside that, the data folder was reorganized into the saves/ and layouts/ structure that still underpins the game, and things like lives, points, and shield stats moved into those files too.

Then came the map system — the birth of the builder. The old "glorified save file" map parser was torn out and replaced with a real one that could create, save, and load your own maps, starting with platforms, staircases, and walls. Once maps existed, the game grew the infrastructure to live in them: a pause menu and a statistics menu across all modes, auto-save, in-game sound downloading on first launch, and character selections that finally saved properly.

The last stretch of V1 was about making it feel solid to move through. Jumping onto platforms and staircases, recoded jumping and falling, fall damage, turn-in-place controls, and a keyboard layout unified across modes turned the builder from a place you could construct into a place you could actually play. By 1.9, characters, weapons, and maps all lived in plain files, the builder could place and save real geometry, and version.txt — the great-grandparent of today's `build/version.txt` — had appeared to track it all.

Everything from 2.0 onward is expansion on this foundation.


SimpleFighter — the V2 era (2.0 → 2.9)

V2 is where the builder came alive. V1 had laid down the skeleton of the map system — platforms, staircases, walls — and V2 spent nine versions filling it with a vast palette of things to place. Almost every kind of object the game is known for today first became buildable and writeable to a map here: doors, signs, clocks, calendars, spikes, items, health zones, blockages, timed text, travelpoints (including ones that carry you to entirely different maps), and a whole layer of audio objects — music, sound sources, reverb effects, and zones. Shields, which had only been half-parsed at the end of V1, were finished and made fully usable.

Just as important as what you could build was how you could build it. V2 turned the builder from a write-only tool into a real editor: you could now add, edit, and remove individual lines in a map (with big contributions from Nikita K), and the map updated live to reflect every change instead of needing a reload. A long-standing map-duplication bug — the one that kept spawning files with doubled extensions — was finally killed, and map-name and invalid-file error checking were added around it. The spy cursor matured alongside, gaining accuracy on multi-tile objects and learning to announce doors, hazards, signs, spikes, travelpoints, and blockages.

Under the hood, V2 deepened the file-driven contract that V1 started. Characters, weapons, and shields each gained per-file sound toggles, so a piece of content could declare exactly which sounds it has and keep borrowed sounds from cutting off. Characters picked up the level and experience modifiers and a default shield; weapons gained a weight stat that feeds into movement. Deprecated stats like jump height and speed were retired from parsing. The input system was rebuilt too — the old virtualizer replaced with a proper text-input class with typing sounds, keyboard themes, and real validation — and the sound downloader was overhauled to extract in-game with progress beeps and a self-cleaning zip.

If V1 answered "can this be a platform?", V2 answered "can you actually build a world with it?" — and by 2.9 the builder had the object palette, the live editing tools, and the audio depth to say yes.


SimpleFighter — the V3 era (3.0 → 3.9)

V3 did two big things at once: it nearly finished the builder's object palette, and it taught the game to perceive its own world. On the building side, almost everything still missing became placeable and writeable to a map — the creatures (animals, enemies, robots, zombies) and their spawn zones, projectiles and projectile zones, bombs and bomb zones, turrets, item zones, vehicles, teleporters, checkpoints, spawnpoints, conveyor belts, hazards and moving hazards, moving and vanishing platforms, and passages. By the end of V3 there was very little you couldn't drop into a map.

One of those additions is worth calling out: in 3.6, instruments arrived — "originally part of another project I was working on, but decided to refactor it a bit and port it over just for the hell of it." You could build a tile and play piano and drums on the keyboard. It was later removed, and it is the very feature brought back in 14.6, more than a hundred versions later. The camera tricks documented in this recent cycle have the same roots: move-focus-with-camera and playing the camera's tile both first appeared here, in 3.6 and 3.8.

That camera-and-spy work was the other half of V3. The spy cursor escaped the builder to work in all game modes, you could now face up and down, and the camera grew a set of senses — speaking its own coordinates, playing the tile under it, and detecting nearby hazards and reverb spaces by ear, with its sounds separated from everything else so they wouldn't muddy the mix. This is the ancestor of the whole modern camera and sonar toolkit.

Underneath, V3 settled foundations that still hold. The map parser switched its separators from colons to spaces — the format the game still uses — with the underscore-for-multi-word-names convention that goes with it. The reverb engine finally learned to handle multiple zones at once, closing out the "one effect at a time" limitation that had stood since V1. Points-menu upgrades became persistent, writing attack, defence, health, ammo, ranges, and shield stats back to their files. And a steady stream of quality-of-life landed: number-row weapon quick-equip, adjustable menu skip amounts for long lists, sound-device selection, clipboard copy and paste of raw map data, and the "go" prompt that replaced the old cheats menu.

If V2 made the builder a real editor, V3 made it nearly complete — and gave the player the senses to explore what they had built.


SimpleFighter — the V4 era (4.0 → 4.9)

If V3 finished placing objects and gave the player senses, V4 made the built world dangerous and alive. The big theme is behavior: the game's threats stopped being passive scenery and started acting. A level-based system was added to everything that can attack or move, so NPCs scaled in strength; animals, enemies, robots, and zombies learned to chase you when they spotted you, with configurable horizontal and vertical sight and attack ranges; and the hazards themselves grew teeth — hazards and force fields now hurl you back in the opposite direction on impact instead of just chipping your health, and mines, time bombs, bombs, and projectiles were changed to damage anything in range, lockable doors included. Mines, time bombs, force fields, lifts, and lockable doors and passages (with passwords) all became buildable and spyable. A heart-rate monitor even started beating faster as your health dropped.

Just as big was the leap in how you author all this. The typed-value era began to end: a new slider class replaced typing numbers for volume, pitch, and reverb parameters — you now dial them with the arrow keys and Tab, with home and end jumping to the extremes and values announced as percentages. And 4.5 brought official sound pack support, one of the most important modding features in the game's life: you could now build your own sound packs spanning all six folder types (characters, equipment, menus, NPCs, objects, and typing themes), with sub-packs, organized however you liked and dropped in at the top level instead of being locked to the single main sounds folder. The old layouts folder was replaced with a cleaner preconfigured-stats folder for every character and piece of equipment (thanks to the blind insanity).

Under the surface, V4 was also a big cleanup. The builder script was folded into the fighter script and the scattered NPC includes were consolidated into a single classes file, cutting a lot of duplication. Long-standing movement bugs were finally put down — moving platforms that knocked you off as you boarded, staircases that misbehaved when you faced up or down, falling off map boundaries — and stacked low platforms could now be climbed like mini-staircases. Placeholders were laid for things still to come: bosses, crushing clamps, and floor breakers.

And it is here, amid that cleanup, that the original instrument was cut. It had been added back in 3.6 as a ported side-feature, but it was welded to a buggy wall-and-platform tile and, more to the point, it was 1,652 lines of hand-mapped copy-paste — one block per note, unmaintainable. In 4.8 it was eliminated entirely, not to be seen again until a clean-room rebuild more than a hundred versions later in 14.6.

If V3 asked "can the player explore this world?", V4 asked "can the world push back?" — and by 4.9, between chasing enemies, knockback hazards, and a heartbeat that quickened as you bled, it could.


SimpleFighter — the V5 era (5.0 → 5.9)

V5 is where the game became a real, shareable project — and not just in spirit. This is the point where the dev started making git commits, the true beginning of open-sourcing it: everything before V5 survives only in the changelog, but from here the history lives in the repository too.

Technically, V5 gave the game two of its defining modern systems. The first is the slash-command prompt: the old "go" prompt was replaced with a real command line (5.4) where you type slash-prefixed commands and /help lists them, and by 5.9 it had grown teeth — /kill and /killall, /give and /getall, /go to move any entity and not just yourself, /relmap to reload instantly. The second, and maybe bigger, is the sound-pack-aligned file structure. In 5.5 every character, shield, and weapon file was moved out of the old data layout and into the sounds tree — the default character now living at sounds/default/characters/default/info.sif, the fist at sounds/default/equipment/weapons/melee/fist/info.sif — so an object's stats sit right beside its sounds. That info.sif-inside-the-sound-pack layout is the one the game still uses today, and it is why a sound pack can carry its own characters and weapons.

The world itself grew in V5, too. Sub-zero navigation arrived (5.6): maps could now extend into negative coordinates, with the builder asking for a minimum as well as a maximum length and height. NPCs got genuinely wall-aware — entities could no longer path straight through walls, and instead of freezing when blocked they would shuffle back and forth as if confused, only chasing when they had a clear line and giving up once you had fled far enough. The wall class itself was split into normal and destructible versions. Bosses, teased across 5.0 and 5.1, became a real buildable entity, and helpers arrived — friendly NPCs that fight for you, damage bosses, and collect items on your behalf. Safe zones and floor breakers became buildable as well.

Under the hood, V5 moved the game's whole interface onto the audio-forms foundation — the settings menu, the documentation reader, and the reverb builder were all rebuilt on it, and the old text-input class was replaced by the virtual_dialogs class that still handles most prompts. And in a small change with a long shadow, 5.0 fixed weapons to respect your actual attack stat instead of blindly multiplying damage by it — the first real shaping of the attack-to-damage relationship that the attack and defence modifiers would refine all the way out in 14.6.

If V4 made the world push back, V5 made the game something you could hand to other people — a documented, command-driven, pack-moddable project with its history finally being written down in commits.


SimpleFighter — the V6 era (6.0 → 6.9)

V6 gave the game a body — and consequences. Up to now your character was a position that took damage; in V6 they became fragile and embodied. The headline is the bone-breaking system: fall from a great height and you can now break bones, several at once, with the odds rising the higher you fall. Broken bones make moving, aiming, and using items harder; animals and zombies can shatter them in a fight; and enough of them will cost you a life or kill you outright. To cope, V6 introduced the game's first real usable items — a health scanner to check which bones are broken and a healing cast to mend them one at a time — turning the items menu from decoration into survival gear.

Enemies grew up alongside you. The old enemy class was rebuilt and renamed "humans," and instead of a generic attack they now equip and fire the game's actual weapons at you — your own arsenal turned against you. Melee weapons gained a reflection mode (press T) that can bounce an attack back, and humans can use it too. And the numbers behind all this got serious: critical hits were added to every entity that can hurt you, attack and defense scaling was reworked, and a damage floor was put in so a hit can never drop below 1 damage when your defense outclasses its attack — the same principle, and the same combat math, that the attack and defence modifiers and the high-level damage fix would return to in 14.6. The combat log arrived here too (press shift E), showing what hit you, for how much, and the experience each kill earned — the very menu that got its level-up spam thinned out in 14.6.

The buildable roster kept filling: security cameras, bikes, elevators with their floor menus, and command blockers all became placeable. Vehicles were rebuilt from scratch around realistic gas-and-brake-pedal movement, with accel and brake speeds. And the slash-command system matured into something fluent — short-hand aliases like /cm and /nm, and the ability to chain several commands at once separated by semicolons.

Underneath, the migration onto audio forms rolled on, pulling the blockage, elevator-floor, menu-zone, spawnpoint and other builders onto the shared validation path, and a scattering of long-standing camera, staircase, and movement bugs were finally put to rest (with more testing help from Parker Watson).

If V5 made the game shareable, V6 made it hurt — a body that breaks, enemies that wield your own weapons, damage that scales and crits, and a combat log to read the wreckage afterward.


SimpleFighter — the V7 era (7.0 → 7.9)

V7 is the great pivot — the version where SimpleFighter stopped being a game with a builder attached and became a builder, full stop. Its very first line says it plainly: "Removed the entire game menu. This game will now be a pure map builder moving forward." The original arena and survival modes that the whole project had grown out of were cut, and everything from here re-centers on authoring and sharing maps. (Those arcade roots were not gone forever — the arena would be revived seven major versions later in 14.0 — but for now the game had chosen to be a tool.)

With that decision made, maps became first-class, shareable artifacts. The maps folder was completely rebuilt so every map is its own self-contained folder with its own info file and its own assets — dialogs, music, NPCs, objects, and sources all living per-map, while characters, equipment, menus, and misc stay global and shared. On top of that came the ability to compile a map into a single distributable file and play it from a compiled-maps menu, plus a template creator that scaffolds a ready-to-customize map for you. This per-map, compile-to-share structure is the direct ancestor of the map format and the map server the game uses today.

Around that core, V7 fleshed out the player and the tooling. A stamina system arrived to govern movement speed, upgradeable and refillable with its own items and heal zones. A proper inventory menu (press I) replaced the old categorized-healing scheme, letting you browse, use, and drop items. Weapon reloading became realistic — it takes time now, with an optional reload ping — and weapon and shield unlock levels were finally honored. Human enemies gained a defense stat that soaks weapon damage, the "armor" concept the attack and defence modifiers would build on in 14.6. Script keys let you bind the number row to your own slash-command sequences, with command stacking, cooldowns, and new commands like /play, /speak, and /suicide. And the camera learned to drop selection points (hold G and press I, J, K, or L) — the very selection markers documented in 14.6.

The buildable roster kept growing too: destroyable platforms and staircases, aircraft with beacons and turbulence, basic switches, and vanishing hazards. And in a piece of infrastructure that quietly changed everything, V7 gave the game a real auto-updater — a version checker, a platform-aware downloader for Windows and Mac installers and portables, 7z extraction, write-permission handling, and cleanup — so the game could finally ship new versions to players from inside itself.

If V6 made the game hurt, V7 made it a workshop — a self-updating, map-compiling, script-driven builder that had set aside being a game in order to become the thing you make games with.


SimpleFighter — the V8 era (8.0 → 8.9)

If V7 declared the game a builder, V8 built the builder's modern toolkit — the menus, systems, and navigation tools that still define it today. The build menu got its category structure here: submenu navigation with the arrow keys, an alphabetical "all" tab that auto-focuses so you can start building immediately, and objects sorted into the audio, construction, interaction, kombat, misc, traps, and other submenus the game still uses. Character blockers landed in interaction, fires in traps, and the whole thing became something you navigate by category rather than one long list.

Two of those systems are the ones a modern map is unimaginable without. The sonar arrived in 8.5 — ping the world around you, with tracking modes, looping speed, and directional control — and the grappling hook in 8.6, letting you pull yourself up and down through the map. These are the exact tools the game shipped help topics for in 14.6, and they were born here. The camera grew a dexterity mode too (hold G, press Y) so you could examine the world hands-free, with its own configurable detection sounds.

Underneath, several systems became extensible rather than hard-coded. The reverb builder was unified into a general effect space that also handles echo and lowpass, with sliders finally showing real values in milliseconds and hertz — the same effect-space system the instrument's audio would later plug into. The help system was generalized so any help file you drop in shows up automatically when you name its topic, and script keys were reworked into custom macro packs you can create and switch between. Together those are why, years later, adding a new builder object in 14.6 meant its help topic just appeared and its sound pool just slotted into effect spaces — the extensibility was laid down here.

The building blocks kept refining, too. Platforms, staircases, and walls were recoded to take health and destroyable directly, retiring their separate destroyable variants, and falling hard on them now wears them down. Walls could be made transparent, platforms and staircases could overlap, and excludable and timed ambiences and sources joined the audio submenu. Some things were retired along the way — the heart monitor (too annoying), sitting and standing, standalone map themes (folded into each character's sound set) — trimming the game toward what it wanted to be.

If V7 chose to be a workshop, V8 stocked it — a category-driven build menu, extensible help and macros, unified effect spaces, and the sonar and hook that turned an audio map into something you could truly feel your way through.


SimpleFighter — the V9 era (9.0 → 9.9)

V9 is the great consolidation — the version where the game's sprawling, separate systems collapsed into the clean, data-driven architecture it still runs on today. Two enormous threads run through it, and both ended with the game far simpler underneath than it had ever been.

The first is the unified NPC system. For years, animals, humans, robots, and zombies had been four separate builders, with bosses and turrets bolted on as their own categories besides. V9 merged all of them into a single NPC system: one builder with category and subtype lists, one NPC zone covering any creature, and — crucially — per-creature info.sif files living beside the sounds, so tuning a wolf's health or a villager's temperament became a file edit (or a form field) instead of rebuilding a map line or hand-coding a class. Into those files went a deep behavior vocabulary that grew all era: a targets list so rival packs fight each other, terrain restrictions with per-tile charge speeds, chase and wander and patrol modes, wounded and healing states that make prey flee and recover, minion launching, teleportation, and a universal defence stat that gives every creature the armor humans first got in V7. By 9.9, armed humans even fired real bullets and projectiles through the exact same weapon code the player uses, so a sniper rifle in enemy hands actually outranges a pistol. Bosses and turrets, having handed all their tricks to the NPC system, were retired as separate ideas entirely.

The second thread rebuilt how you author all of it. Nearly every builder in the game was converted to the tabbed audio-form layout — every field shown at once, with inline sound previews and live volume and pitch sliders, instead of the old one-question-per-screen flow — and the build menu itself became a row of category tabs you flip through. This is the exact build-form experience the game still uses, the one a new object like 14.6's instrument simply slotted into. Effect spaces grew from a handful of effects to eight, gaining highpass, chorus, distortion, phaser, and flanger with a proper help topic. And a raft of systems that 14.6 would later lean on arrived here: obscurity zones for hiding entities from the spier and info menu, object tracking to home in on a target, item-based lock modes for doors and passages, and a from-scratch refactor of projectiles into clean bouncing hazards.

It is hard to overstate how much of the modern game crystallized in V9. The NPC info.sif contract, the audio-form builders, effect spaces, obscurity zones, the kombat category, weapon fields like stun mode and launch type, even the bike fixes — nearly every system touched in 14.6 took its present shape here. If V8 stocked the workshop, V9 rebuilt the workshop's foundation so everything in it spoke the same language.


SimpleFighter — the complete story so far (V1 → V9)

Across nine major versions and a little over two years, SimpleFighter transformed from a hard-coded arena shooter into a mature, data-driven, self-updating engine for building audio games — and it did it entirely in two dimensions, right up to the edge of the 3D port that begins in V10. What follows is the whole arc of that 2D era in one piece.

It began, in V1, as a survival shooter with everything baked into the code: a fixed arena, bouncing bullets, vehicles, an upgrade menu, and reverb audio. The first real work was pulling all of that content out of the code and into editable files — characters and weapons given their own attribute files, and the map system born as a way to author your own platforms, staircases, and walls. V2 and V3 filled that skeleton in: nearly every object the game is known for became placeable and writeable to a map — doors, signs, clocks, audio sources, creatures, zones, vehicles, checkpoints, passages — while the builder itself grew real editing tools and the player gained senses, a spy cursor and a camera that could read the world by ear. (Instruments even made a brief first appearance here, in 3.6.)

V4 gave that built world danger: enemies that chase with configurable sight and attack ranges, hazards and force fields that hurl you back, and the arrival of official sound-pack support that let anyone build and organize their own audio. V5 is where the project opened up in every sense — the first git commits, the slash-command prompt, and the decision to move every character, weapon, and shield into the sound tree as info.sif files, the structure the game still uses. V6 gave the player a body with consequences: a bone-breaking injury system, usable healing items, a combat log, and enemies rebuilt into "humans" who wield your own weapons against you.

Then came the pivot. In V7, the original game modes were cut and SimpleFighter re-founded itself as a pure map builder — maps became self-contained, compilable, shareable folders, and the game grew an auto-updater to ship itself to players. V8 stocked that new workshop with its modern toolkit: a category-driven build menu, extensible help and macros, unified effect spaces, and the two navigation tools that define moving through an audio map, the sonar and the grappling hook. And V9 consolidated it all — merging every kind of creature into a single info.sif-driven NPC system and converting nearly every builder to the tabbed audio-form layout, so the entire game finally spoke one language underneath.

By the end of V9, SimpleFighter had become something coherent: a moddable, command-driven, pack-based audio game engine where content lives in plain files beside its sounds, every builder shares one form-based UX, and the world is navigable entirely by ear. It had shed its origins as a game to become the thing you make games with — and it had done all of it on a flat plane. V10 is where it reaches for the third dimension.


SimpleFighter — the V10 era (10.0 → 10.9)

V10 is the third dimension. For its entire life the game had lived on a flat plane — maps had width and length, and "height" was just how high you could jump — and V10 gave it a real z axis, then, with remarkable discipline, walked every single system across into it. This is not a burst of new features so much as a methodical migration: the same game, now with up and down.

The port was done category by category, entity by entity, in a deliberate order. The platform went first, in 10.0 — taught to sit at a real height so you could fall to it, jump up to it, and walk under or over it without colliding — and walls, staircases, and text zones followed as volumetric regions you move through on z. From there the migration marched through the whole build menu: the zones, then the audio and construction objects, then the transitions (doors, passages, elevators, lifts, moving and vanishing platforms), then vehicles, bikes, and aircraft, then the traps and interaction objects, and finally the kombat category — NPCs, weapons, and bullets — brought across last in 10.9. By the end, every buildable object knew its height, gained a z field in its builder, and gated its behavior so an object on one floor of a 3d map no longer reached through to another floor sharing its x and y.

The player's senses and tools were ported right alongside the objects. The spier learned to read depth and height, reporting volumes as X by Y by Z and staying quiet about entities on other floors. The camera gained full 3d position readouts — in front and behind for depth, above and below for height — and 3d-aware markers. The sonar learned to scan up and down on z and to keep lateral pings to your own floor. The grappling hook learned to throw straight up on the height axis. Weapons and bullets gained a z range and could fire in every direction a character can face, so an enemy on the floor above you now shoots straight down. Even movement was rebuilt: plain W and S became your up-and-down step keys on 3d maps (the weapons and shields menus moving to alt+W and alt+S to make room), mirroring page up and down.

Two things make V10 as much a feat of restraint as of engineering. The first is backward compatibility: throughout the port, 2d and topdown maps kept working untouched — z fields exist on those entities but are simply never consulted, and every old map still loads. The second is that V10 finally pinned down the game's three map modes as distinct, first-class ideas — 2d for a side view with jumping, topdown for a bird's-eye plane with no height, and 3d for the full x, y, and z — with each entity, each key, and each readout behaving correctly in all three. Topdown even got its own careful polish, hiding the objects that only make sense with a height axis and relabeling directions to front and back.

Everything the 14.6 instrument port relied on to speak fluent 3d — the mapmode-locked parser with its z-field variants, the camera and sonar and hook that already knew about height, the weapon range fields the new move fields would mirror — was made real here. V10 did not add a dimension so much as teach the entire game to think in one.


SimpleFighter — the V11 era (11.0 → 11.9)

If V10 gave the game a third dimension, V11 gave it a direction to face — and then grew up its combat and its menus around that. Three big threads run through it.

The headline is body rotation. Before V11, movement and aiming on a map were world-aligned: up was always north, left was always west. V11 gave your character a real heading on topdown and 3d maps — q and e rotate you 45 degrees at a time — and then rewired the entire game to orient around it. The arrow keys now walk relative to the way you are facing, aiming points where your body points, and the sonar, the spier sweep, and the camera-move keys all follow your heading too. Most striking, the whole soundscape rotates with you: a sound on your right slides to your left when you turn around, looped sounds re-panning frame by frame as you spin. It is the change that turned an audio map from a set of fixed compass directions into a place you move through first-person. (2d maps, where the vertical axis is altitude rather than depth, stayed world-aligned.) Much of the rest of 11.0 and 11.1 was finishing the 3d port's rough edges — settling which keys mean what in each mode, prefilling builders from the camera markers, making tile lookups height-aware, and refreshing every help topic for the new x, y, and z fields.

The second thread poured depth into combat. Melee weapons stopped firing invisible traveling bullets and became instant strikes on the nearest valid target inside your facing half of the range box, able to break the same destructible map elements bullets could, with a swing-speed wind-up and a scadder field for splash and multi-target hits. Weapons and shields grew a rich vocabulary of per-file knobs: melee, reflection, and refchance to author deflectors; scadder for splash; swing speed for wind-up; launch type so a weapon or a shield can fire a projectile or drop a bomb; and blockchance and cushion so a shield behaves like body armor with occasional gaps. Custom weapon categories became as easy as making a folder. And TTS enemies returned — the old version had shelled out to a command-line tool that lagged the whole map, so they were rebuilt on the engine's native voice path, each with its own voice, rate, volume, and pitch, wired into the spier, the object menu, the tracker, and obscurity zones like any other entity.

The third thread rebuilt the menus. The entire menu system was moved off the old m_pro engine and onto the new form-menu layout the game still uses, with the build and weapons menus becoming tab strips you flip through — a category row across the top and an all tab that aggregates everything alphabetically — and locked weapons and shields finally pushing back with an error instead of silently doing nothing. The settings and in-game preferences menus were trimmed down to only the toggles that actually do something.

A great deal of what 14.6 took for granted was set here: the form-based menus, the tabbed build menu the instrument's category sits in, the weapon-field authoring culture the new move fields extend, the facing system those move fields follow, and TTS enemies as a first-class damage source. V11 is the version where the 3d game stopped feeling ported and started feeling native.


SimpleFighter — the V12 era (12.0 → 12.9)

V12 turned the game into an economy and a scripting platform, and along the way gave its files the shape they still have. Several deep threads run at once.

The first is a real combat economy. The old points menu became a tabbed shop, and ammo stopped being an abstraction: every non-melee weapon now names one of sixteen ammo types, and reserves became a character-wide pool per type, so two battery weapons draw from one battery stash you refill at the shop's ammo tab and upgrade at its damage tab. Firing itself gained a price — new stamina drain, health drain, and life drain fields let a weapon cost the person pulling the trigger part of their stamina bar, their health, or even a life card on every shot, and because the cost falls on the firer, an armed human wielding the same weapon pays it too. Combat also learned to shove: push x, push y, and push z fields knock a target a set number of tiles per axis (a negative value pulls them toward you for a tractor beam), with extra damage and its own kombat-log line when the shove slams someone into a wall. That push system is the direct parent of the move fields added in 14.6.

The second thread made the map scriptable. Switches, until now little more than a toggle with a sound, gained on and off command strings that can run any slash command — including stacks separated by semicolons with millisecond delays — so a single switch can teleport you, sound an alarm, gate a story beat, or spawn an ambush. Two new commands backed this up: /build places an object inline from its on-disk fields with no dialog, and /spawn does the same in memory only, for temporary effects that vanish on the next map load. Command stacking was made non-blocking so a delayed sequence no longer froze the game, and a raft of commands gained direct-name arguments and confirmation-skipping flags for use inside macros.

The third thread reorganized the game's entire on-disk structure into what it is today. Each map's single info file was split into main.sif for the parser data and meta.sif for its owner, description, and dates, with new map-menu options to manage all of it. Weapon, shield, and npc folders were reorganized to keep their info in a data subfolder and their clips in a general one, matching characters; the docks and data folders were split into main and builder buckets; and the top-level sound-pack layer was removed entirely, flattening audio paths down to sounds/main and sounds/builder. Map files even gained line-comment syntax so a line could be hidden from the parser without being deleted. Nearly every path and naming convention the 14.6 work relied on — meta.sif, the flattened sound tree, the data-and-general split, the spaced info.sif keys — was finalized right here.

And for sheer novelty, V12 added two wild weapon archetypes driven purely by folder name: the glider, any weapon starting with glider, which you hold Ctrl to fly across the map (burning stamina as fuel, invincible while aloft, and dealing contact damage to whatever you fly into), and the cloner, any weapon starting with clone, which copies the entity it hits. If V11 made the 3d game feel native, V12 made it deep — an economy to manage, a scripting surface to build with, and the file layout the rest of the game's life would be authored on.


SimpleFighter — the V13 era (13.0 → 13.9)

V13, the last major version before 14, is where the game became fully authorable — and fully documented — from inside itself. Its work was less about new mechanics than about making everything already there editable, rebindable, undoable, and explained.

The centerpiece is the in-game data managers. A new data manager menu gathered add, edit, remove, and view tools for every character, weapon, shield, and NPC, so authoring a creature's stats or a weapon's fields became filling out a form instead of hand-editing an info.sif in a text editor. Alongside it, the character data layout was simplified from four files to two — the attack-verb and body-part lists folding into main.sif beside the stat block, and later renamed to break verbs and body verbs to sit cleanly next to the stats. This is the exact two-file character layout, and the exact main.sif, that 14.6's attack and defence modifiers live in and that its field reorder tidied.

Authoring the game's controls and settings grew up too. A config/keyboard.ini file arrived with full in-game rebinding — add, edit, remove, and view any key binding — and the settings menu became a tabbed layout with its own speech tab for configuring the SAPI voice, rate, pitch, and screen-reader output. Map editing gained undo and redo, so /undomap and /redomap step back and forth through your recent adds, edits, removals, and clipboard pastes.

The builder itself was unified. Every object that used to ship separate single and ranged entries — clocks, signs, doors, hazards, spikes, travelpoints, and the rest — collapsed into one builder entry with a build-mode choice at the top, the map line deciding single or ranged simply by how many coordinates precede the text, and text fields moved into quotes so messages, item ids, and passwords keep their exact spacing. This is the single-or-ranged pattern, and the quoted-field format, that a new object like 14.6's instrument slotted straight into. New entities kept coming as well — sensors as a contact-triggered switch, trampolines, stereo speakers, fall zones, streaming url sources, and saveable effect-space presets — but they arrived into a builder that now spoke one consistent language.

And above all, V13 documented the game. A sweeping push across its last few versions added an in-game help topic for nearly every entity and system in the builder — doors, hazards, platforms, bikes, zones, audio sources, and dozens more — turning the game self-explanatory. It is the reason 14.6 could add an instrument help topic and have it simply appear alongside the rest. If V12 made the game deep, V13 made it legible: everything editable through a form, bindable to any key, reversible with a command, and explained in its own words.


SimpleFighter — the V14 era (14.0 → 14.6, the current era)

V14 is the reunion — the version where the game becomes two games again. For seven major versions, ever since V7 cut the original modes to make SimpleFighter a pure builder, it had been a tool for making games rather than a game itself. V14 brought the classic arcade survival mode back — but not as the hard-coded arena of 1.0. This time it rides on top of the entire mature content engine the intervening years had built: the 270-plus weapons, the unified NPC system, the three map modes, the sound packs. You pick a map mode and an enemy type, drop into a flat arena, and fight endless scaling waves, with safe corners to retreat to, a twenty-slot save system, on-the-fly field resizing, a per-slot shop, and normal or custom wave modes. The action game and the map builder finally coexist on one foundation, and the readme was rewritten to greet new players as an action game first and a builder second.

Around that reunion, V14 kept enriching and connecting the builder. New entities arrived — slants that ramp you up and down, tile zones and flux zones that reshape how the ground and your stats behave as you cross them, and winds that shove you around like a fire that pushes instead of kills. The builder was linked to the wider world through a map server: you can now download maps others have shared, upload your own for the server owner to moderate, and, as that owner, approve or reject them from inside the game. And it became self-auditing and browsable — a two-tier map error checker that flags any broken or nonsensical line instead of silently dropping it, a sound gallery for auditioning every element's audio by category, and templates that drop a whole couch or table into a map in one action.

The era closed, in 14.6, by bringing instruments home. The keyboard piano-and-drum toy that had been cut back in 4.8 for being an unmaintainable hand-coded mess returned as a clean, data-driven builder object — this time with a recorder it never had before — alongside a run of refinements: a fix for high-level enemy damage that had been silently collapsing to nothing, new weapon move fields for charge-and-recoil attacks, per-character attack and defence modifiers, and a quieter level-up that stops burying you in chimes and log spam.

It is the era where every thread of the game's history finally sits together in one program — the arcade roots reawakened, the builder deeper than ever, the third dimension woven through all of it, and the modding engine underneath — and it is still being written, with 14.6 the latest chapter.


SimpleFighter — the modern era (V10 → V14.6)

If the first nine versions built a flat, moddable audio game engine, the five that followed gave it depth, polish, and — finally — its old self back. This is the story of that second half.

It opens with the single largest undertaking in the game's history: the 3D port of V10. A real height axis was added, and then every system in the game — every buildable object, every sense, every combat interaction — was walked across into it, category by category, while 2d and topdown maps kept working untouched. By the end, the game's three map modes were pinned down as distinct, first-class ideas, and the world had gained an up and a down.

V11 taught the 3d game to face a direction: a body-rotation system on topdown and 3d maps that reoriented movement, aiming, the spier, the sonar, the camera, and even the stereo soundscape around your heading, turning the game genuinely first-person. It also poured depth into combat — instant melee, a rich vocabulary of weapon and shield fields, TTS enemies rebuilt on native voices — and rebuilt the entire menu system onto the tabbed form layout still in use.

V12 made the game an economy and a scripting platform. Per-type ammo pools and a tabbed shop turned resources real, weapons learned to cost stamina, health, or lives to fire and to shove targets with a push system, and switches became general triggers that run any command. Underneath, the whole on-disk structure was reorganized into the layout the game still uses — main.sif split from meta.sif, the flattened sound tree, the data-and-general folders — and two wild weapon archetypes, the glider and the cloner, arrived.

V13 made all of it authorable and documented from inside the game: data managers for editing every character, weapon, shield, and NPC through forms, keyboard rebinding, undo and redo for map edits, a unification of every object into a single build-mode form, and a sweeping push that gave nearly every entity its own in-game help topic.

And V14 brought the reunion. The arcade survival mode that V7 had cut came back — this time riding the whole mature engine — so the game is both a playable action game and a map builder once more. Around that, the builder gained a map server for sharing, a two-tier error checker, a sound gallery, and templates, and the era closed by bringing instruments home in 14.6, the hand-coded feature of the V3 and V4 days reborn clean.

Where V1 through V9 answered "what is this game?", V10 through V14.6 answered "how far can it go?" — into a third dimension, into a real economy and script surface, into full in-game authoring, and back around to the arcade game it started as, all on one engine.


SimpleFighter — the whole story (V1 → V14.6)

On the 26th of June, 2024, SimpleFighter's first version was a hard-coded arena shooter: a fixed field, bouncing bullets, and everything baked into the code. A little over two years and more than a thousand commits later, it is a fully three-dimensional, moddable, self-documenting audio game engine that is also, once again, a playable arcade game. This is the whole arc, a paragraph each.

The first nine versions turned a game into an engine. Content was pulled out of the code and into editable files; a map system was born and then filled with nearly every object the game is known for; the world gained danger and the player gained a body that could break; the project opened its source and settled on the file structure it still uses; and finally, in a decision that defined everything after, V7 cut the original game modes and re-founded SimpleFighter as a pure map builder. V8 and V9 stocked and unified that builder — the sonar, the hook, effect spaces, and a single data-driven NPC system on the tabbed form-based interface the game runs on today.

The next five versions gave that engine depth and its old self back. V10 added a third dimension and methodically ported every system into it. V11 gave the 3d game a facing and rebuilt its combat and menus. V12 made it an economy and a scripting platform and locked in its final file layout. V13 made everything editable and explained from inside the game. And V14 brought the arcade survival mode back, so the action game and the builder finally live together on one foundation — closing, in 14.6, by bringing the instrument full circle: added as a hand-coded toy in 3.6, cut for being an unmaintainable mess in 4.8, and reborn clean, with a recorder it never had, more than a hundred versions later.

That last story is the whole game in miniature. What began as a small, hard-coded arena shooter became a platform sturdy enough that a feature once thrown away could return as a handful of clean, data-driven files that simply slot into place. From a flat plane to three dimensions, from a game to an engine and back to a game — one developer, fourteen major versions, and counting.
